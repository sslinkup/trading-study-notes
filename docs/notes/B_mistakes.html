Mistakes, Anti-Patterns and Risk

Original study notes. The linked videos are the original sources and remain their authors' property. Click a timestamp to jump to the relevant moment.

1. The common failure patterns

Most losses can be traced to a short list of behaviours rather than to a missing indicator: trading too large, trading too often, refusing to take profit, moving stops, and treating a lucky outcome as skill. The cure is unglamorous — know your limit, stay inside it, and collect data on yourself until the patterns are visible.

The classic blow-up sequence is a strong run that inflates confidence, a period of careless sizing, a deep drawdown, and then a re-deposit made with the feeling that the market will eventually turn. Recognising the sequence early is more useful than predicting the market itself.

Sources: Things You Should Not Do in 2025 ([05:05], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mPMn_g20SHU); Do Not Make These Mistakes Trading in 2026 ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CXwXDa-9YCs).

2. Bankroll and concentration

Start with an amount whose loss would not change your behaviour. If a small stake cannot be grown, a larger one will not fix the underlying problem. Keep bet size constant; changing size after a win or a loss is one of the fastest ways to turn a process into a gamble.

Avoid using the whole account on a single idea. Full-size swings carry the same practical risk as high leverage, even without leverage. Over-diversification is the opposite error: holding dozens of coins makes it impossible to follow any of them. A handful of instruments that you know well is preferred.

Sources: Things You Should Not Do in 2025 ([05:05], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mPMn_g20SHU); The Basic of TA — Setting Up Charts ([38:36], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zv8pXI_7VJY).

3. Accept that it is gambling

The source material is explicit: an entry is a bet on an unknown outcome. Calling it an investment or a profession does not change that. What can be improved is the quality of the bet — the size, the invalidation, the target, and the decision process — not the certainty of the result.

Sources: Lesson 1 — Introduction to Trading ([03:31], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKJPwG14y2M); Things You Should Not Do in 2025 ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mPMn_g20SHU).

4. Taking profit

A winning position that becomes a losing one is a self-inflicted error. The recommended habit is to close part of a trade once it is in profit rather than waiting for a perfect exit. A partial close of roughly 60–70% is described as a reasonable default; 50% is more aggressive. Once part has been banked, the remaining position can be given a wider stop, because it is being carried by the result rather than by hope.

Do not be angry about taking profit. There will be another trade. If a trade is closed too early, the response is to plan the partial close in advance next time, not to replay the decision emotionally.

Sources: Lesson 3 — Trade Strategy ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8WAUnwtRBE8); Lesson 5 — Leverage, Taking Profit, Thinking Ahead ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UscLoywpZR0); Key Thoughts Going Into 2026 ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ti3JhXEY9UM).

5. Happy decisions and emotional leaks

The practical test for a decision is whether it leaves you calm. Trades opened out of boredom, revenge, or fear tend to share identifiable features — a familiar coin, a familiar time of day, a familiar mood. Find those recurring situations in the diary and remove them one at a time.

The trader controls the button. No chart, indicator, or commenter places the order. Framing decisions as choices you own makes it easier to accept both good and bad outcomes as information rather than as verdicts on your worth.

Sources: Mastering Yourself ([01:10], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gJNO11fkK_Y); Do Not Make These Mistakes Trading in 2026 ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CXwXDa-9YCs).

6. Stop losses as invalidation

A stop is not a dollar amount you are willing to lose; it is the price at which the reason for the trade stops being true. Placing it by account size rather than by chart structure is the most common mistake.

Use recent swing wicks and key areas to decide where the idea breaks. If price moves above a wick that marked a rejection, the thesis is wrong and the exit is justified even if the loss feels large. Conversely, if the structure is intact, a stop placed only to soothe emotion is likely to be hit and reversed.

Sources: Guide to Stop Losses — Introduction ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yNhmxEc6y5c); Key Thoughts Going Into 2026 ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ti3JhXEY9UM).

7. Liquidity and stop hunting

Market makers push price toward obvious clusters of stops. Horizontal levels that everyone can see attract price, which is why stops placed just beyond a well-marked level are often swept before the move continues. Draw the level, but expect the wick through it.

Sources: Do Not Make These Mistakes Trading in 2026 ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CXwXDa-9YCs); How to Use Aggr.Trade (2025) ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KPD2ElzbQnc).

8. Entry timing and overtrading

Entering late is a recurring error: the trade is opened after the move has already happened, which leaves no room for the stop. Wait for the pullback or the confirmation the story requires.

Set a daily limit on attempts. Three unsuccessful entries in a session is a common stopping point. Continuing beyond that is usually an emotional decision rather than a technical one.

Sources: Part 1 — Timing Your Positions ([04:48], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NyGIkmMb-Sw); Lesson 3 — Trade Strategy ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8WAUnwtRBE8).

9. Keep the toolkit small

A recurring warning is against indicator collecting. The core toolkit is RSI, stochastic RSI, a few EMAs, Ichimoku, and Keltner/Watson levels — used together to tell one coherent story. Adding more tools usually adds more excuses, not more edge. The three-minute chart in particular is overused; higher timeframes provide the context that makes a lower-timeframe entry meaningful.

Sources: Lesson Number 5 — Stochastic RSI and Keltner Channels ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7MmCcxadAwQ); How to Approach Timeframe ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VoIr0w6l8gc).

10. Study, security and adaptability

Journal the reason for every decision, including the ones you are unsure about. Do not copy another trader's entries without understanding the invalidation; a copied position cannot be managed when the story changes. Keep exchange balances low and hold long-term assets in self-custody.

Finally, stay willing to be wrong about a market view. The willingness to update a thesis is what separates a flexible trader from one who keeps forcing an outdated story onto new price action.

Sources: The Key to Success ([18:08], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UfxEboq-tOM); Key Thoughts Going Into 2026 ([00:00], https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ti3JhXEY9UM).
