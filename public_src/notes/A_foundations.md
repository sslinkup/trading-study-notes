# Foundations: Mindset, Bankroll and Journaling

*Original study notes. All prose here is written from scratch; the linked videos are the original sources and remain their authors' property. Click any timestamp to open that moment in the source video.*

## 1. Trading as a game against yourself

The starting idea is that the market is an arena in which the main opponent is the trader. Outcomes are uncertain, so the useful question is not "will this win?" but "did I make a good decision under uncertainty?" Most participants are expected to lose, and the way to avoid that group is not a secret indicator but better decisions and better money management.

A second framing is that this is gambling analysis. Nobody knows the next price. Each setup is like a door leading to a different possible future, and once price has moved through a door, that branch is closed. The trader's job is to estimate which branches are more likely, act on that estimate, and manage the downside when reality takes another path.

Effort, not account size, drives improvement. Depositing more money does not make a trader better; deliberate practice does. The early months are for learning with small size, not for searching for a 100x.

## 2. Start small and learn your own statistics

The first account should be deliberately small — tens or hundreds of units rather than thousands. The reason is practical: you cannot learn position sizing, emotional control, or win rate from a size you are afraid to lose. A useful test is whether you can turn a very small stake into several times its size before adding capital; if you cannot do it small, adding size usually magnifies the same mistakes.

Win rate must be measured, not assumed. Knowing that a trade won is not enough; you need to know why it won, what the average win and average loss look like, and how the number was produced. A trader who cannot state their own average bet size has a capital-management problem before they have a strategy problem.

The blow-up pattern is well known: a lucky run turns a small account into a large one, a few careless trades give most of it back, and the trader re-deposits expecting the market to "owe" them. Avoiding that arc matters more than finding the next entry.

## 3. Data on yourself: journal and diary

Two separate records are recommended.

**Trading journal.** A log of the trades themselves: entry, stop, target, size, reason for the trade, what happened, and what was learned. A 90-day challenge is suggested as a starting block, then another, and then the practice continues indefinitely. The journal tells you whether the rules are being followed.

**Trading diary.** A record of the trader, not the trades: mood, energy, sleep, hours worked, day of week, emotional state at the time of the decision. The purpose is to find recurring leaks. If losses cluster on certain days or in certain moods, that pattern is more actionable than another chart pattern.

Decisions can be mapped as decision trees. For each situation, list the possible responses and the likely emotional consequence. The aim is to identify the specific choices that lead to an unhappy outcome and remove them one by one, rather than trying to fix everything at once.

## 4. Bankroll rules

- Keep bet size constant. Changing size after a win or loss is how a good process turns into gambling.
- Use a separate, untouchable long-term holding portfolio and a dedicated trading portfolio. Mixing them leads to risking money that was meant to be safe.
- Avoid "full stack" swings. Putting the entire trading account on one outcome is comparable to high leverage even when no leverage is used.
- Do not over-diversify. A small number of well-understood positions is preferred over dozens of coins the trader cannot follow.
- The poker reference point: at a $1/$2 cash game, a serious bankroll is around $20,000, so a $200 loss is a small fraction. The same proportional thinking applies to trading size.
- A personal martingale variant is described in the source material but explicitly presented as an experiment, not a recommendation.

## 5. Leverage and position size

Leverage is treated as a money-management choice rather than a strategy. Ten times leverage on a large account and one hundred times leverage on a small account can control the same notional exposure; what changes is how much room the position has before liquidation and how the trader feels about the number on screen.

The psychological ceiling on size is real. Scaling from tiny trades to large ones changes decision quality. Increase size only after the smaller size has been handled consistently, and keep the step small enough that a losing streak does not change behaviour.

## 6. Keeping the money

Making money and keeping it are different skills. The rule is that profit taken from a trade should be moved out of the exchange and into a wallet the trader controls, rather than recycled into the next position. Withdrawal discipline is what converts a good run into a lasting result.

Daily goals should be concrete and achievable. If the day's target is a modest amount, take the profit when it appears instead of holding out for more and watching the trade turn into a loss. A completed small target beats an open-ended hope.

## 7. Rules, checklists and planning

The best traders cited in the material all work from written rules — not only exit and stop rules, but rules about when to trade, when to stop for the day, and how many attempts to make. A checklist turns those rules into something repeatable under stress.

Trades should be planned before entry: the invalidation level, the target area, the size, and the story for why the setup exists. The phrase used in the sources is to write the story rather than react to the story. If the story changes after entry without a structural reason, that is usually a sign to exit rather than to justify holding.

A three-strike convention appears in the lessons: after a small number of failed attempts in a session, stop and come back later. The point is to prevent tilted, repeated entries from turning a small loss into a large one.

## 8. Learning routine

Ask specific questions. Instead of "is this a good trade?", ask what would invalidate the idea, which timeframe is being traded, and what the higher timeframe says. Verify claims against the source video rather than memory — that is what the timestamp links are for.

The training horizon is measured in months, not days. Practice across different market regimes, because a trader who only sees a bear market or only a bull market will be unprepared when the character of the market changes. Adaptability is treated as a core skill: being willing to be wrong about a read is what allows the read to be corrected.
